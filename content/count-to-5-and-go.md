### Learning to move, learning to live

When a fish lives its whole life in a small bowl, it never learns what waves feel like.
We are different. We can choose to leap.

For a long time, I felt the edges of my own bowl before I ever found the courage to step beyond it.

Each decision carried me further from what was familiar, and closer to something I couldn’t yet name.

![somewhere in the night, hiking, in a magical place that felt from another world](../pictures/el-teide-at-night.jpeg)

This is a story about crossing that invisible line—about how small choices, made quietly and repeatedly, can reshape a life.
About learning to stand on my own—not out of necessity, but out of choice.

It began simply.

I moved out of my parents’ home and, almost by accident, found my way into sport. Running was the easiest place to start. It required nothing but a pair of shoes and the willingness to begin.

But something was missing.

I could run in my hometown, yes—but there was no energy around it. No sense of movement, no people to learn from, no quiet push to become better. And I’ve always been the kind of person who needs inspiration before discipline can take root.

Everything shifted when I moved to a mountain city.

![Cluj-Napoca, Romania](../pictures/cluj-landscape.jpeg)

---

The landscape itself seemed to invite motion. The mountains changed the way I saw the world—and myself. I felt freer, more independent, and, for the first time, genuinely hungry to move.

Hiking and running became a habit. Then came cycling, which completely changed me.

As an adult, discovering cycling felt like opening a hidden door. It wasn’t just about riding—it was about distance, exploration, and possibility. Bike touring, bikepacking, mountain trails—I stepped into all of it gradually.

First short rides. Then longer ones. Half days. Full days. Then weekends away. And eventually, journeys that stretched beyond what once felt reasonable.

Before I fully understood what I was doing, I was planning a bicycle trip from Timișoara, Romania, to Split, Croatia—with people I had only just met.

![Rides that changed my mind forever](../pictures/cycling-rainbow-in-front.jpeg)

It was difficult. And it was deeply rewarding.

There was joy, but also fatigue. Long roads, border crossings, moments reduced to the basics: food, water, rest. And somewhere in all of that, I became more responsible—more aware of what I truly need, and what I can let go of.

---

### Fifty kilometers at a time

They say the first night in a tent is the hardest.
They’re right.

We camped somewhere random—far from roads, lights, and any trace of people. Just a quiet patch of land near a sunflower field, reached by a narrow side road. It felt like a good idea at the time.

The moment we started setting up camp, the mosquitoes came. Fast, aggressive, everywhere. Darkness was already settling in, and we had no time to hesitate. Everything had to be done quickly, almost mechanically.
I was still learning. My friends carried most of the process, moving with a kind of calm efficiency I didn’t yet have.

By the time we finished, night had fully arrived.

And with it—noise.

Every sound felt amplified. Rustling in the grass, distant footsteps, voices carried from somewhere I couldn’t see. That was the beginning of my first white night—a night without sleep.

Even though the air was warm inside the tent, fear settled into my body. My legs felt numb, as if they no longer belonged to me. I lay there, eyes open, listening, imagining, waiting.

I didn’t sleep.

But strangely, I was still happy to be there.

Morning became something I longed for with an intensity I had never felt before. And when the first light finally appeared, it felt like relief.

![Get out of your comfort zone as much as you can](../pictures/tent-by-the-river.jpg)

For me sleeping in a tent in a wildspot after cycling all day long, being tired and dirty was not something easy but it teached me a lot and I started to give up to many things I once considered necesarly.

---

I stepped outside, curious now instead of afraid, trying to make sense of the night.
A few meters away, three rabbits were playing in the field. Calm, unaware, almost indifferent to my presence. Maybe they had been the source of all that noise.

Or maybe my mind had done the rest.

They say rabbits sleep with their eyes open. After that night, I believed it.

A new day began—and with it, hunger.

Our days quickly found a rhythm. We were always thinking about the essentials: water, food, distance. We cycled early, from around 7 until noon, trying to cover as much ground as possible before the heat became unbearable.

By midday, the sun took control. Harsh and unforgiving, it forced us to stop.

Those hours—between 12 and late afternoon—felt like a pause in the journey. We would hide in a city park, stretch out on the grass, nap, eat something simple. Sometimes we allowed ourselves a small luxury: a cold lemonade, a place to charge our phones, a moment of stillness.

Then, around 16:30, we started again.

The second part of the day always felt lighter. My body had already accepted the effort, and the kilometers came more easily. We rode until evening, until the light softened and the air cooled.

---

Before stopping, we would pick up food for the next morning. And then came the familiar, slightly desperate task: finding a place to sleep.

A safe place. A quiet place. A place that felt hidden enough.

I often wondered how I would handle that moment alone.
The uncertainty, the exposure, the silence.

Having someone with you changes everything.

### Golden Lands to Blue Horizons

Serbia surprised me with its simplicity.

Endless sunflower fields stretched in every direction—golden, quiet, almost hypnotic. Cycling through them felt different than any other way of traveling. On a bike, you don’t just pass through a place—you absorb it. The smells, the subtle shifts in temperature, the texture of the air itself.

It felt complete.

The beauty of it was not dramatic, but steady. It filled me with a quiet kind of energy. Life there seemed to move at a different pace. Villages were nearly empty, and over the course of three days, we saw only a handful of people. Mostly, it was just us, the road, and the open land.

![Wild campspots that will always leave a memory](../pictures/bike-in-random-campspot-wild.JPG)

Flat, rural, repetitive—and somehow deeply calming.

At times, it felt like meditation.

---

One afternoon, as we stopped to rest, I noticed a small fire starting in the fields nearby. The heat had dried everything to the point where it took almost nothing for flames to appear. Without thinking too much, I grabbed water and tried to put it out.

Maybe it would have gone out on its own.
Or maybe it wouldn’t have.

Either way, that moment stayed with me—the quiet realization of how fragile everything around us was.

As we continued south, the landscape began to change.

Bosnia came and went almost like a transition between worlds. The dry, golden fields slowly gave way to green—dense, alive, full of movement. Forests, flowers, birds, even deer crossing in the distance. Signs of life were everywhere, along with the steady presence of farming.

![Green paths of Bosnia and Herzegovina ](../pictures/biking-croatia.jpeg)

We didn’t stay long. We were just passing through.

But the shift was enough to feel.

And then came Croatia.

The closer we got to the coast, the more intense everything became. The heat grew heavier, almost physical, pressing down on us with every kilometer. We chased shadows wherever we could find them—small patches of relief that felt like rewards.

![Cycling self-sustained Romania-Croatia](../pictures/bikepacking.jpg)

Water became everything.

---

I don’t think I’ve ever been so aware of my surroundings as I was during those days. Cycling strips life down to its essentials. You feel everything more directly—the sun, the effort, the thirst, the relief.

And strangely, I was almost never hungry.

I ate because I knew I had to, because my body needed it. But what I truly craved was something else entirely—freshness, lightness, water in its most natural form.

Fruit became an obsession.

Especially watermelon.

![Hapiness is simple.](../pictures/watermelon-love.jpeg)

### Between Fear and Wonder

Crossing borders by bike felt strangely unreal.

We were always the only ones doing it—surrounded by cars, engines, noise—yet moving at our own fragile pace. There was something exposed about it, but also freeing. At each crossing, we were waved through with a kind of quiet priority. It was quick, almost too easy for something that felt so significant.

Until it wasn’t.

The second time we entered Croatia, everything changed in a moment.

Out of nowhere, five dogs came charging toward us—loud, fast, aggressive. Barking, chasing, closing the distance. Fear hit instantly, sharp and physical. I didn’t think—I reacted. My legs moved faster than I thought possible.

Adrenaline took over.

I could have been exhausted seconds before, and suddenly it didn’t matter. I pedaled with everything I had, my body flooded with energy I didn’t know I could access. Somehow, the guy we were with managed to push them back, to create just enough space for us to escape.

And just like that, it was over.

But the feeling stayed.

![So afraid and tired.](../pictures/hot-climbs.png)

### Adrenaline and Grace

Not long after, the road tested us again.

We had reached the Croatian coast—serpentines, cliffs, the sea somewhere beyond sight. Beautiful, but demanding. And then, a mistake.

We entered a tunnel.

I still had my sunglasses on.

---

It felt like riding straight into blindness—into a dark, echoing space filled with the distant roar of cars. I couldn’t see. I could only hear. Engines passing on the left, air shifting around me, my entire focus reduced to one thing: stay straight, keep moving.

Stopping wasn’t an option.

My friends turned on their bike lights, but it barely helped. It felt like lighting a match in the middle of a forest and hoping it would guide you out.

So I kept going.

Pedaling through fear, through noise, through uncertainty—until, finally, light.

We emerged.

Alive. Safe. Shaken.

And in that moment, something shifted.

I felt an overwhelming gratitude—not just for getting through it, but for things I had always taken for granted. Vision. Movement. Breath. The simple ability to exist and experience the world.

We all say we’re grateful.
But sometimes, it takes fear to make you actually feel it.

### Moments that stay

Later, near Split, the world softened again.

We passed through a small village, tired, dusty, and running low on water. When you cycle for long distances you move fast and it's a lot of dynamic around you and I feel like when you need something you won't find it, it comes when you stop thinking about it, hence at this moment we were happy to meet this guy along the way who seemed to have the best time of its life.

![Free horse having lunch by a sideway street](../pictures//sweet-random-horse.jpeg)

We couldn’t find a store, so we asked a few locals if they could help us refill our bottles.

They looked at us—really looked.

Dirty. Exhausted. A little lost.

And then something beautiful happened.

One bottle of water turned into slices of melon.
The melon turned into a full meal.
The meal turned into cappuccino.

Then their children came home.
And suddenly, we were part of their evening.

We talked, laughed, shared stories. They were curious about us—about what we were doing, why we chose this kind of journey. There was warmth, generosity, a kind of openness that didn’t ask for anything in return.

By the end of it, we had shared a barbecue together.

Complete strangers, for just a few hours—connected in a way that felt simple and rare.
That night, we searched again for a place to camp.

And we found it.

A forest that didn’t feel real at first. Dense trees, dry leaves covering the ground like a soft carpet, silence that felt almost intentional. We stepped into it as if entering another world.

We set up the tent.

And when darkness came, the forest came alive.

Fireflies.

---

Dozens of them, maybe more—glowing, moving, surrounding us. Small, quiet lights floating in the dark. It felt unreal, like something imagined rather than lived.

We watched in silence.

And then, sometime in the middle of the night, I woke up.

I’ve always been a light sleeper. I heard something—soft steps, careful movement. Fear came first, as it always does. But this time, curiosity followed.
I stepped outside.

And there it was.

A deer. A male, still and alert, standing not far from the tent.
Surrounded by fireflies. Lit softly by the moon.

For a moment, everything stood still.

![Natural lights in the forest](../pictures/fireflies.png)

No fear. No movement. Just presence.

It was one of the most beautiful things I had ever seen.

### Kindness Along the Way

For a few seconds, I couldn’t move.

I stood there, caught between fear and something close to wonder. The deer passed right beside our tent—silent, calm, almost unreal. I watched it without breathing, afraid that even the smallest movement would break the moment.

Then it was gone.

And all that remained was the feeling.

The next morning, reality hit quickly.

We packed up and left without breakfast. By the sixth day, moments like this were no longer unusual—we simply rode until we found what we needed. Hunger had a different meaning on the road.

Eventually, we reached a small store, bought some food, and sat down on a curb in the parking lot, next to our bikes.

A man noticed us.

He walked over slowly, curious, smiling. A Croatian grandfather, still cycling himself, proud of it in a quiet, genuine way. He tried to speak English, searching for words, but it didn’t really matter.

We understood each other anyway.

Sometimes, presence is enough.

Water became our universal language.

In one village, we stopped in front of a house and tried to ask an elderly woman for water—using gestures, pointing at our bottles, smiling, hoping she would understand.

She did.

But she gave us more than we asked for.

---

Her face lit up with excitement as she spoke in her own language, pointing at our bikes, then at herself, as if sharing something we couldn’t fully translate. Maybe she had once known this kind of freedom. Maybe she simply recognized it.

She filled our bottles, watched us with warmth, and stayed there on the steps as we left.

Looking at us with kindness.

Another time, we stopped again—just for water.

A woman and her child were in front of a beautiful house. We asked, expecting the same simple exchange.

But it turned into something else.

She invited us to sit down.

Water became sweets.
Sweets became coffee.
Coffee became beer.

We stayed longer than planned, cooling down after hours of heat and effort, surrounded by a quiet generosity that asked for nothing in return.

These moments stayed with me more than the kilometers.

### The Day I Chose Myself

As we approached the sea, we knew we were getting close to something special.

Plitvice Lakes National Park had been on our list from the beginning—a place where we would finally stop, rest, and reset.

![Camaraderie, friendship, company, sharing same passion](../pictures/camaraderie.jpg)

After six days of cycling around 100 kilometers a day, we needed it.

We found a proper campsite. Showers. Stillness. Time.

It felt like luxury.

The village nearby was quiet, green, almost unreal in its calmness. For the first time, I took the luggage off my bike and went for a short ride with no pressure, no distance to cover.

Just movement for the sake of it.

There were trails everywhere, forests inviting you in, a sense that you could stay longer, explore deeper.

I felt at peace.

But my friends were already thinking ahead.

The next morning, they wanted to continue.

That’s when I realized something important.

I was tired.

Not just physically—but deeply, fully tired. My body was asking for a break. I hadn’t been sleeping well, my energy was low, and I could feel it clearly.

So I made a decision.

I stayed.

They continued toward Montenegro and eventually back to Romania. And I remained in Croatia—with just 200 kilometers left to reach Split.

For the first time, I was on my own.

It was the beginning of my first solo travel experience.

---

Split, the beaches, small islands, quiet walks, time to explore without a plan. It was different—slower, more personal, more honest.

And it felt right.

That decision taught me something I won’t forget:

There is no fixed way to continue a journey.
Sometimes, the strongest choice is to pause.

If you feel too tired, too unsure—stop.

Listen to your body. Your intuition.

Because on the road, everything depends on it. One moment of distraction, one second of imbalance—and the consequences can be real.

You don’t have to prove anything.

![Enjoy the journey, appreciate the company.](../pictures/on-the-road-again.png)

### Does It Hurt?

Yes, it does but it won't matter!

Your body will feel it first. The saddle becomes a challenge of its own. Your hands go numb, searching constantly for a new position on the handlebars. Muscles tighten, resist but eventually adapt.

You learn quickly that preparation matters.

Stretching before and after the ride is no longer optional. Starting the day with a climb without warming up—something you only do once. After that, you remember.

You adjust.

You learn to take care of yourself in ways you didn’t have to before.

And slowly, your body changes.

---

It becomes stronger—but also more honest. It tells you when to push, and when to stop.

And if you listen carefully, it will carry you further than you thought possible.

- Missing luggage? –

It was pouring rain, we barely could hear each other and the lockers were somewhere outside. We solve this and look for a bus.

---

Her flight was scheduled sooner so she had to leave before me. I could have spent a few more hours there, I went to the bridge and I was heading to some gallery, so happy and chill.

When I got a message from Sophia saying:
‘Hey, my locker was open, my bag is missing, I don't know anything about yours, the locker SEEMS closed.

At this point I discovered that there are many phases of anxiety:

panic - my face becomes warmer, the entire body starts pulsing fast.
terror - cash, laptop, ID card, payment cards would have been gone.
resignation - eventually the most important thing that I’m still in one piece, still have passport and some cash.

It’s fine, I’ll start everything again..

Everything stopped for a few minutes, I was in such a nice bus, nice streets, good food/ snacks..

Nnow I didn’t want anything, anymore, no food, no drinks, no walks, no bridge.

---

I just wanted to teleport there and feel the heavy backpacks.

Why on earth I didn't take my laptop, wallet and that’s it. I was checking with ‘Find me’ whether the laptop has a different location, it was updated 8’ ago, still at the airport. But should I trust this?

At this point with all the negative energy that I accumulated, I succeeded in messing up the bus stations. I waited for 10 minutes on the wrong side. It's literally 30m further away from the bus station, on the same sideway.

Then I moved to another bus station, on the wrong side of the road, then I lost the bus because of not waving.

I learned this from Madeira, but at that moment I totally forgot, then I had to wait another 15’ for the next bus… Great!

Decided to get Bolt, 2 drivers rejected me.

The third one arrived with difficulties, but eventually he arrived and it was good that I did it like that because he dropped me exactly at the Lockers.

---

I rushed there. I was visualising the luggage is still there. I hoped until the last second, although I was so afraid.

Code worked, bags were there - ALL GOOD.

- carrying bags by yourself -

When traveling alone you need to be strong and fast.

Don’t want to be mean - but I cannot help noticing other girls I see traveling and they are ‘my size’.

Out of the blue I see them showing up in some bus station with a trace of a backpack

And then some guy arrives... carrying 2 huge backpacks and barely breathing.

Big thumbs up for these guys, but, oh man.. Sometimes I wish I had that. So selfish.

Then comes the part where maybe you wanna go to the toilet and you kinda need to take everything with you, no?

My solution is to be very minimalistic, but now I also have faith in people that they don’t mind my luggage.

---

Sometimes when I go to toilet, I leave things outside and I pray nobody notices.

– when you go alone for food you cannot try too many dishes -

Come on, let’s face it!

When you go with somebody, you get what you can share!

But when you’re alone you don’t do that, you get only what you KNOW you will really eat.

Also when doing some trips on hop-ons from one place to another, IF you have the chance to team up with somebody they count up like ‘2/5 pax’ which is always more affordable and fun.

- Feeling ‘lonely’ -

OH, you would say this is likely to happen because you THINK you are most of the time alone, no?

---

But from my perspective, this is simply impossible, if you choose to stay in a hostel.

You don’t have time to feel alone.

Not necessarily because there are people everywhere, but because you become BUSY, busy to learn, busy to discover, every day feels like entering a new portal that pushes you through a new world.

You need to nevigate, you need to interact and get something.

Personally, I started to take notes and study what draws my attention in the spare time - when I was not working.

It’s an endless journey, every day becames a wonder...
