Is Romania Safe for Solo Female Travellers? An Honest Answer

Romania · 9 min read

I grew up in Romania. I know the country in the way you can only know somewhere you spent your first twenty-something years — the real texture of it, not the tourist version. So when I see travel forums debating whether Romania is safe for solo women, I usually read a paragraph, close the tab, and move on. The answers tend to be either too reassuring or too dramatic, and neither is useful.

This is the honest version. Not designed to sell you a trip or scare you off one.

The short answer

Yes, Romania is safe for solo female travellers — including in the cities, including in the mountains, including if you're travelling without a fixed plan. Violent crime against tourists is genuinely rare. The things that make some people uncomfortable are different: catcalling, staring, occasional unsolicited advice from older men about where you should or shouldn't go. Real, but not dangerous.

The long answer is that "safe" is a spectrum, not a switch, and different parts of Romania feel different depending on who you are and what you're doing.

The cities

Cluj-Napoca is the city I know best — I moved there from Craiova and it felt like a different world. Young, educated, international in feel even if not in demographics. Walking alone at night in the centre is fine. Taking taxis or rideshares after midnight is fine. The kind of low-level harassment you sometimes get in big cities exists, but it's not the defining experience of being there.

Bucharest is louder and more chaotic and some parts feel different at night than they do in daylight, as is true of almost every capital city in Europe. That's not a reason not to go — it's a reason to pay the same attention you'd pay in Lisbon or Warsaw after dark.

Sibiu, Brasov, and Sighisoara are very walkable, very touristed in summer, and very easy for solo travel. They're the kind of places where you can sit alone at a cafe for two hours and no one will bother you or even look at you twice.

The mountains

This is where my personal experience is deepest, and where I'd push back hardest against the idea that Romania is only for group travel. The Carpathians are spectacular, the trail infrastructure is better than people expect, and the hiking community is genuinely welcoming.

Turda Gorge, Romania. This canyon is one of the best things in the country and almost no one outside Romania knows it exists.

The practical concern people raise is bears — Romania has the highest brown bear population in Europe outside Russia, and they're real. But hiking alone in the Retezat or the Bucegi or the Apuseni mountains is something Romanian women do all the time. You make noise on the trail, you don't hike at dusk, and you trust your instincts when they tell you something is off. The same things you'd do in any mountain environment with wildlife.

What's less practical: the attitude in some smaller mountain towns that a woman hiking alone is somehow suspicious or in need of saving. I've had men — well-meaning, genuinely concerned — offer to accompany me on trails I've done dozens of times because they couldn't quite believe I was doing it on purpose. It's annoying, not dangerous. You learn to be politely firm.

What to actually watch out for

Scams at Bucharest's train station and airport — classic tourist-trap taxi overcharging, which you avoid by using Bolt or Uber like everyone else. Not a solo female issue, just a tourist issue.

Driving in Romania if you're not used to it. The roads vary enormously between motorways and rural routes, and driving culture is aggressive by Western European standards. Take buses and trains where you can in the countryside — they're cheap and the views are good.

Stray dogs in rural areas. Less of an issue than it was a decade ago but still worth being aware of if you're hiking or cycling in the countryside. Walk calmly, don't run, give them space.

Attitudes in very rural, traditional areas — where a woman travelling alone may be genuinely puzzling to local people, not in a threatening way but in a who are you and where is your family way. This can feel uncomfortable if you're not expecting it. It's usually accompanied by extraordinary hospitality once people decide you're okay.

What makes Romania actually good for solo travel

It's significantly cheaper than Western Europe, which gives you more flexibility in where you stay and what you do. Budget solo travel is genuinely possible in a way it isn't in, say, Scandinavia.

The landscape is varied in a way that rewards slow travel. Mountains, medieval towns, the Danube Delta, vineyards, monasteries — you can spend a month and not repeat yourself.

Romanians are, in my completely biased opinion, some of the most genuinely helpful people I've encountered anywhere. Not in a performed-for-tourists way — in a you look lost, let me walk you there way. I've experienced this in my own country and in twenty others, and Romania is still in the top three.

And there's something specifically good about visiting a country as a woman when you have some understanding of how it actually works — where the safe neighbourhoods are, what the real transport options are, when to trust your instincts and when you're just being unfamiliar. Romania rewards that kind of attention.

The actual answer

Go. Take the normal precautions you'd take anywhere. Know that the country is safer than its reputation and rougher around the edges than the Instagram version. Expect to be surprised, occasionally frustrated, and more often than not genuinely delighted.

Romania is not a country you visit and leave unchanged. That's the thing about it that nobody puts in the safety guides, and maybe the most important thing I can tell you.
